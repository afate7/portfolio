Over a year ago, I was between things, and I got introduced to an opportunity in the GIS domain.

I'd never worked in the field before, but I thought it would be just another domain to learn. A new set of tools, a few new terms, then you move on. I mean, how hard can it be for someone who's worked 13 years in digital products to understand GIS?

Turns out: **very.** It almost completely reset my baseline for "hard."

For the uninitiated, GIS is the technology that connects data to location. It lets us stack layers of information, traffic, weather, sales, onto a map to make decisions. It's a field with deep roots (dating back to the 1960s) and massive players dominating the industry, like **Esri**, alongside open-source heroes like **QGIS**.

## So what makes GIS different?

It's not that it's *different*, exactly. It's more like someone decided to escalate the situation for no particular reason. When you try to apply modern software thinking to GIS, it fights back.

**The design challenge.** You're not just building a dashboard. You're trying to display layers of reality, something genuinely complex, without overwhelming the user. (Good luck with that.) You can't design a map interface the way you'd design a banking app.

**The tech challenge.** Standard databases don't work here. Spatial data demands an entirely different approach to storage, querying, and processing. You can't treat coordinates like text in a table.

**The complexity.** In GIS, the complexity isn't accidental, it's not legacy systems or regulation. This is *domain* complexity, where the problem itself is mathematically brutal. GIS shares more DNA with aerospace navigation and game engines than it does with typical products.

## So why am I still here?

Where you might see those challenges as cons, I see them as pros. Honestly, this amount of complexity is intriguing to me. But that's not all.

**The impact.** Building maps to mitigate flood risk. Evacuation planning. Service-territory optimization, school coverage, utilities planning. Things that would be extraordinarily hard, if not impossible, without GIS.

**"Where" matters.** There's a layer of intelligence that only exists when you visualize location. Patterns jump out on a map that are invisible in a spreadsheet. You see connections you'd never find otherwise.

**The opportunity.** During my market research to really understand the industry, one thing became clear fast: the space is growing steadily, around 12% CAGR, and it feels like GIS is only a few steps from mainstream. Not just for governments or smart cities, but for small shops, startups, and everyday businesses too.

At the same time, the market still feels fragmented. There are powerful tools everywhere, but not enough clear segmentation, and a real gap in user experience. Which means there's huge room for better products and smarter solutions.

## And to anyone considering this path

If you get the chance to build in GIS, take it. It will fundamentally change how you think about systems, data, and problem-solving.

Finally, a shout-out and huge respect to everyone working in GIS. Thank you for building, and suffering silently, in this field. For squeezing your brains daily to create experiences that change how the world operates. The work is mind-bending, and I know now that the headaches are real. But I think it's worth it.
