There's a UI pattern I see everywhere. You sign up for a service, and somewhere in the flow — buried in a wall of text, or pre-checked in a form — is a checkbox that opts you into something you didn't ask for. You didn't notice. You didn't consent. But now you're subscribed.

This is a dark pattern. And we — designers, developers, product managers — built it. We A/B tested our way to it. We shipped it on purpose.

> "Dark patterns are design decisions that benefit the business at the direct expense of the user."

I've been thinking about this a lot lately. Not because dark patterns are new (they've been documented since at least 2010), but because they've become so normalized that many designers implement them without a second thought. We optimize for conversion without asking: *conversion of what, for whom?*

## The catalog of manipulation

Dark patterns come in flavors. Some are brazen — the classic "roach motel" where signing up is frictionless but canceling requires calling a phone number during business hours and speaking to a retention specialist trained to make you feel guilty.

Others are subtle. The **"confirmshaming"** dismiss button that reads *"No thanks, I don't want to save money."* The countdown timer on a discount that resets every time you reload the page. The interface that buries the privacy settings six layers deep, in a font that's technically readable but requires genuine effort.

Each of these is a design decision. Somebody wrote that button copy. Somebody placed that checkbox. Somebody ran the test that said this version converts better and shipped it.

## The business case we keep making

"It works" is the defense I hear most. And it does work — in the short term. Friction-less sign-ups increase free trial numbers. Pre-checked boxes increase newsletter lists. Confusing cancellation flows reduce churn.

But here's what the A/B test doesn't measure: the user who tells three friends about the experience. The chargeback dispute. The tweet thread that goes viral. The erosion of brand trust that compounds slowly, invisibly, until it doesn't.

**Trust is a long game. Dark patterns are a short one.**

## What we can do instead

The good news is that transparent design isn't just ethical — it's often more effective. Users who genuinely understand what they're signing up for convert more durably. They have lower churn. They refer friends. They complain less.

Here's what I try to do in my own work:

- Write every CTA and label as if the user's lawyer is watching.
- Default to the option that's best for the user, not the business.
- Make the "no" as easy as the "yes."
- When I feel tempted to hide something, ask why — and fix the underlying problem instead.
- Measure long-term retention, not just conversion.

## A simple test

Before you ship a UI decision, ask yourself: *if my user fully understood what this interaction was doing, would they feel good about it?*

If the answer is no — if your design relies on confusion, speed, or inattention to work — it's a dark pattern. It doesn't matter if it A/B tested beautifully. It doesn't matter if the PM signed off on it. It's still manipulation.

We built this thing. We can build it differently.
