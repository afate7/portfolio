Research that ends in a findings deck changes nothing. The Define phase existed to convert everything Phase 1 saw into **problem statements a business can be held to** — each one pinned to the step it lives on, the number it costs, and the metric that will judge the fix.

## Step 1 — One wall per journey step

**Deliverable: four problem walls — Homepage, Basic Data, Quote List, Payment.**

Each wall followed the same skeleton: the Hotjar and GA evidence at the top, then *problem → our thinking → goal → metric*, then the step's flowchart, then the open questions we owed stakeholders answers to.

![Per-step problem walls with drop-off rates, flowcharts and open questions](/assets/cases/tameeni/stage-problem-statement.png)
*The four walls. Every step carries its own drop-off number like a price tag.*

The numbers, from the Nov 2023 – Feb 2024 window:

- **Homepage** — 1m 44s average, **13% drop-off.** The sidebar (primary navigation) noticed by 1.44% of users; an ID-field tooltip nobody clicked; sections users scrolled past without engaging.
- **Basic Data** — 1m 11s, **11.1% drop-off.** A tab structure that confused new-policy and ownership-transfer users; people editing the retrieved ID number because nothing told them whether they could; tooltips ignored — except the sequence-number one, the single place users genuinely needed help.
- **Quote List** — 4m 39s, **21.7% drop-off.** The worst step, at the worst moment: choosing an offer. The price filter was users' first instinct and hardest find. Quote cards were so overloaded — and so tall — that comparing even two quotes side by side was physically impossible. In a comparison product.
- **Payment** — 5m 35s, **20.2% drop-off.** A confusing policy-details card, a receipt users couldn't parse, an IBAN dropdown that appeared even when the user had no saved IBAN and then couldn't be unselected, and a share feature with a **0.08% click rate** whose copy explained nothing.

## Step 2 — Weight the problems

**Deliverable: a weighted problem backlog, not a wish list.**

Listing problems is easy; the discipline was ranking them. Each problem was weighted by the drop-off it sat inside, the number of users it touched, and the confidence of the evidence behind it. That weighting is why the Quote List — not the homepage everyone had opinions about — became the center of gravity for the redesign. It's also a sticky note on the original process board, in my handwriting: *"and the weighting of the problem."*

## Step 3 — Interrogate the constraints

**Deliverable: a constraints ledger — what's regulation, what's habit.**

Every problem wall ended in questions for product and business, because half of any insurance UX is law and the other half is legacy, and they masquerade as each other. Some answers: login at certain points is **regulatory** — it stays; the IBAN retrieval requirement is **regulatory** — it stays, but its broken presentation doesn't; several "mandatory" quote-list fields existed **for regulatory reporting only** — insurers barely used them, so they didn't deserve prime screen estate. And one behavioral tell we almost misread as a bug: heavy Back-button use on the quote list turned out to be **brokers** comparing on behalf of clients — a real segment the redesign would have to respect rather than design away.
