01
The landscape

Understand what
recruiters want

Before you write a single line, understand the room you're writing into. Recruitment has quietly changed shape, and the biggest change is speed. On average, a recruiter spends about seven seconds on a CV. Your job is to make the match obvious in that window.

Much of the first pass isn't even human. Applicant Tracking Software (ATS) and, increasingly, AI now source, screen, and score candidates before a person ever reads a word. The consequence is blunt: a genuinely qualified candidate can still be filtered out if their CV isn't written to match the posting's language.

The upside is that the same tools work for you. Around two in three HR professionals see AI as a positive in hiring, and you can use AI writing tools as an assistant, to draft a cover letter, tighten a summary, or rephrase a clumsy sentence, as long as the substance stays honest and yours.

What they're actually scanning for

Clear, relevant strengths · impact shown with metrics · evidence you went above and beyond · that you followed the instructions · and that you can adapt. Growing weight goes to cultural fit, diversity, and data-driven selection to reduce bias.

02
Where the jobs live

Find work online

Don't fish in one pond. Google Jobs aggregates listings from across the web and is a good starting point. LinkedIn doubles as your public CV and a jobs board, collect skill endorsements and verifiable references, follow companies, and set alerts; roughly a third of employers recruit through social media. Boards like Reed let you filter by salary, location, and hours, and often bundle courses to upskill.

Cast a wide net across several platforms, including sector-specific ones, to raise your odds of an interview.

One small discipline that matters more than it sounds: when you screen-share or screenshot your search, hide personal bookmarks and information first.

03
The cover letter

Five steps,
one page

About 60% of companies ask for a cover letter, and 83% of hiring managers say a good one can earn an interview even when the résumé is thin. Keep it to one page, and write a fresh one for every role, you can reuse excerpts, but the tailoring is the point. This is a genuine sequence, so follow it in order:

Open with an introduction

Who you are, the skills you bring, why you fit, the exact role, and where you heard about it. Use the reader's name if you have it; otherwise, “For the attention of the Hiring Manager.” Name any referral.

Lead with your most relevant experience

Order by relevance, not date. Open with achievements and numbers, “grew followers 20% in a month” beats “managed social media.”

Show how you'll add value

Connect past wins to this role's responsibilities. No experience yet? Lean on education, training, volunteering, and unpaid work, and your goals.

Include qualifications & education

Dates, institutions, and any relevant certifications or awards (first aid, health & safety, top of class).

Sign off with intent

Polite and actionable, never pushy: “I'd be honoured to be considered, I look forward to hearing from you.”

Cover-letter non-negotiables

One page · mirror the posting's exact words, skills, and job title to beat the filters · flawless spelling and grammar (use a checker) · and spell the recipient's name right.

04
The CV

Build for the
scanner

Keep it to one page (two at the very most), tailored to each job. A CV isn't your life story, it highlights the parts of your experience and character most applicable to this role. Five elements carry it:

  • Basic information, name, contact, general location; add nationality/visa, licence, or portfolio links only if the role needs them.
  • Opening summary, two or three sentences: your elevator pitch. Who you are, what you do for an employer, what you want.
  • Work experience, reverse-chronological, in bullets, with active verbs and achievements over duties. Paid or unpaid both count.
  • Education & achievements, qualifications, institutions, dates, awards.
  • Skills & strengths, and these two are not the same thing.

Skills are what you're trained in and can prove. Strengths are often personality-deep, things you do so naturally you may not notice them. Ask colleagues; they'll name strengths you can't see.

Employers broadly want three kinds of skill, and a real skills gap (about 63% of employers report one) makes evidencing them valuable:

Soft

People skills

Leadership, teamwork, communication, languages, built through hobbies, sport, and volunteering.

Hard

Technical skills

IT, Excel & Office, marketing, accounting, languages, the measurable know-how.

Digital

Online skills

Handling info, communicating and transacting online, staying safe, plus coding, editing, social.

05
Tools & polish

Don't start
from a blank page

Templates get you moving; editing makes you competitive. Ready-made, editable CV templates live in:

Google Docs · docs.google.com Canva · canva.com Jobseeker · jobseeker.com VisualCV Kickresume Craft CV · craft-cv.com

Then sharpen every version before you send it:

  • Lead with results, what you did, how, and the outcome. Use metrics: “increased sales 40% in one year.”
  • Show evidence, link a public article, video, or sample. Never share IP-protected or company-internal files.
  • Use active verbs, led, delivered, coordinated, created, devised, and drop the first person.
  • Tailor efficiently, keep one long master CV and trim it per job description.

The seven-second checklist

Before you hit send

  1. Tailored to this exact posting, not a generic send.
  2. Keyword-matched to the job title and skills, so the filter passes it on.
  3. Proven with metrics, not adjectives.
  4. One page, scannable, active verbs, bullets.
  5. Spotless spelling, grammar, and the recipient's name.
