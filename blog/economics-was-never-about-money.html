Economics Was Never About Money

🧑‍💻
Ahmed Alfateh
February 10, 2025
10 min read Thinking
📊

For years, I dismissed economics as one of those subjects that seemed important but felt irrelevant. Charts. Supply-demand curves. The kind of thing that matters to finance people, not to anyone actually building things in the world.

Then I stumbled on a definition so simple it stopped me cold: economics is the study of choice under scarcity.

That's it. Not markets. Not money. Choice.

Suddenly, everything I'd been doing for years, deciding which features to build, which problems to prioritize, which users to design for first, had a name. I'd been doing economics without knowing it.

The Problem That Never Goes Away

We live in a world where wants are infinite and resources are not. You've known this your whole life, even if you've never thought about it in those terms. Every time you choose how to spend a Saturday, which project to take on, or which meeting to skip, you're making an economic decision. You're allocating something scarce.

Economists call the cost of choosing one thing over another opportunity cost. The real price of any decision isn't the money you spend, it's what you give up to make that choice. Reading this article has an opportunity cost. So does every feature on your product roadmap.

Scarcity isn't just about money. It's about time, attention, energy, and focus. In product development, the scarcest resource is almost never budget. It's clarity, about what actually matters, and why.

The Myth of the Rational Human

For most of the 20th century, economists built their theories around a fictional character called homo economicus, economic man. This person was perfectly rational: always self-interested, always maximizing utility, always making optimal decisions with perfect information.

It's a seductive model. Clean. Mathematical. Predictable. It produced elegant theories about market behavior and consumer choice. There was just one problem: real humans don't behave this way. Not even close.

Enter the rebels.

In the 1970s, psychologists Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky started running experiments that revealed something uncomfortable: humans are systematically, predictably irrational. Not randomly wrong, but wrong in specific, repeatable patterns. Behavioral economics was born, a field that studies how people actually make decisions, not how they theoretically should.

The Insight That Changed Everything

Here's one of the most powerful ideas from behavioral economics, and it's worth sitting with: we feel losses more intensely than equivalent gains.

Kahneman and Tversky called this loss aversion. In experiments, most people prefer a guaranteed $500 over a 50/50 shot at $1,000, even though the expected value is identical. Rationally, you should be indifferent. Emotionally, the thought of losing feels worse than the thought of winning feels good.

This single insight explains a remarkable number of human behaviors:

  • Why people stay in mediocre jobs rather than risk a better one
  • Why investors hold losing stocks too long, hoping to "break even"
  • Why users resist new software, even when it's clearly better than what they have
  • Why governments find it easier to promise benefits than to take them away

If you've ever launched a product and watched users refuse to adopt it despite obvious advantages, you've witnessed loss aversion firsthand. What you're fighting isn't laziness or irrationality, it's a cognitive feature, not a bug.

Predictably Irrational

What makes this field genuinely useful, and what Dan Ariely captured brilliantly in Predictably Irrational, is that our irrationality isn't random. It follows patterns. And patterns can be understood, anticipated, and designed around.

Take relativity. We almost never evaluate options in absolute terms. We compare them to whatever's nearby. This is why a $200 wine seems reasonable next to a $350 bottle on the same menu, and outrageous at a restaurant where everything else costs $20. The wine hasn't changed. The context has.

Or anchoring: the first number you see disproportionately influences every number you consider after it. Show someone a high anchor, and their estimate shifts up. Show them a low one, and it shifts down. This happens automatically, below the level of conscious awareness.

These aren't weaknesses to be ashamed of. They're characteristics to be understood.

Why Any of This Matters

Behavioral economics isn't just an academic curiosity. It's a toolkit for understanding why the world works the way it does, and for designing better within it.

Governments use these insights to "nudge" behavior: making organ donation opt-out instead of opt-in, automatically enrolling employees in retirement savings plans, placing healthier food at eye level in cafeterias. Small changes to how choices are structured produce large, measurable changes in outcomes.

Companies use them to design products that actually get used, and to avoid building ones that get ignored despite being technically superior.

The field has also fundamentally changed how we understand financial markets. Traditional finance assumed prices always reflect all available information, the "efficient market hypothesis." Behavioral finance showed that markets are deeply human: driven by fear, greed, narrative, and crowd psychology. The 2008 financial crisis was, among other things, a behavioral economics event.

The Bigger Picture

What I've come to appreciate is that economics, real economics, stripped of its technical reputation, is fundamentally a story about humans navigating a world that won't give them everything they want.

We are not calculating machines. We are emotional, social creatures who use mental shortcuts, get swayed by irrelevant context, and sometimes make choices that seem to contradict our own best interests. Behavioral economics doesn't condemn this. It documents it, explains it, and, at its best, helps us design systems that work with human nature rather than against it.

The next time someone tells you economics is about markets and money, tell them they're describing the plumbing. The real subject is the people who turn on the taps.
