The Webview Problem

Category
Chapter 02 · The Old App
Year
2024
My role
Research Manager
Team
Design & research team
🪦

Open the old Tameeni app and scroll to the bottom of the home screen. There it was: a footer. Copyright notice, link columns — the unmistakable anatomy of a website, living inside an app store download.

That footer was the most honest thing about the product. The "app" was the website wrapped in a Cordova-style webview: every screen a page load, every transition a browser event, every interaction carrying web latency into a context where users expect native response. The market leader in Saudi motor insurance was shipping its website in an app costume — and by the time a genuinely native competitor arrived, users could feel the difference in their thumbs, even the ones who couldn't name it.

The audit

Before designing anything new, we catalogued everything old. Every screen of the existing product — landing, third-party liability flow, comprehensive flow, policy views, renewal paths — went on the wall, organized by journey.

The full audit of the old product, flow by flow. Web pages posing as app screens — you can spot the footers even at this zoom.

Laying it out this way did something a bug list never does: it made the pattern visible. The problem wasn't a bad screen; it was a wrong species. Forms designed for desktop cursors, tap targets sized for mouse pointers, navigation that assumed a browser back button, and information density borrowed from a website that had never been asked what a phone was for.

Choosing what comes next

The rebrand wasn't a coat of paint on the webview — that would have been repeating the original sin. It started from a moodboard: what should a product feel like when it sells something as anxiety-adjacent as insurance, in Arabic and English, to people who mostly do this once a year under deadline?

The moodboard that set the visual direction — calm surfaces, honest hierarchy, and a brand built for the product rather than borrowed from the website.

The direction we chose traded the website's density for room to breathe: fewer decisions per screen, one primary action at a time, and a visual identity that could finally exist natively instead of arriving through a rendering engine.

The old app told users, on every screen, that they were an afterthought of the website. The new direction's entire job was to reverse that sentence. How the flows themselves were rebuilt — architecture, wireframes, and prototypes — is the next chapter.
